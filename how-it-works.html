From the ledger in the basement to a map on every phone

Four steps. Nobody from our side needs to visit.

Step 1

A 20-minute call

We agree on your section names, which way rows run, and who will take photos.
Step 2

Phone photos

Volunteers photograph each stone with our guide (about 60-90 an hour), and scan the ledgers. No volunteers? We hire a vetted local helper.
Step 3

We transcribe and check

AI drafts every entry. A trained reviewer checks every field against the image, and a second reviewer re-checks a sample.
Step 4

You approve, we publish

You review a private preview, then the map goes live. We host it, back it up and add new burials.
Live demo

A real church cemetery, built from open records

We built a working demo from the City of Niagara Falls' open cemetery data for Stamford Presbyterian Church Cemetery: 1,127 plots and 1,062 searchable names across four sections, with search, filters, monument photos and walking directions.

The privacy rule is built in: 238 recent burials show as private records, and their names never reach the public file.

Why a person checks every field

We tested free AI handwriting tools on public-domain burial ledgers. The best one got only about a third of fields exactly right on a 1900s lot card, and it was confidently wrong almost half the time.

So AI only drafts. A reviewer sees the original handwriting next to the draft and confirms or corrects it. Ditto marks, red-ink corrections and "2 children of Mrs." on one line all need a human.

Anything nobody can read is labeled unreadable. We never guess.
